Bible Basics

A practical starting point for reading Scripture, understanding its books, and exploring foundational teachings.

Study principle: Read what the passage says, notice its context, compare related passages, and distinguish the text from conclusions people draw from it.

A Helpful Reading Pattern

Observe: What does the passage actually say?

Context: Who is speaking, to whom, and why?

Compare: What do other relevant passages add?

Apply: What response does the passage call for?

1. Read the Bible in Context

A verse can be misunderstood when separated from the paragraph, chapter, book, historical setting, or the Bible's wider message. Context does not guarantee that everyone will agree, but it helps keep interpretation anchored in what the author communicated.

Five questions to ask

  1. Who is speaking or writing? Is it a prophet, narrator, poet, Jesus, an apostle, or another speaker?
  2. Who is being addressed? Israel, a particular church, an individual, a ruler, or a general audience?
  3. What is the surrounding argument or story? Read the whole paragraph and the section before and after it.
  4. What kind of writing is this? Narrative, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, Gospel, letter, or apocalyptic vision?
  5. How does the passage relate to other Scripture? Compare clear, relevant passages without assuming every verse uses a word or image in exactly the same way.

Pay attention to literary style

Poetry often uses imagery and parallel lines (Psalm 23). Proverbs generally expresses wisdom principles, not guarantees that every event will unfold the same way (compare Proverbs 22:6 with the variety of life experiences). Parables communicate through a story. Apocalyptic passages use visions and symbols. Letters address real questions and circumstances in early Christian communities.

Example: Philippians 4:13

Read Philippians 4:10-14, not only verse 13. Paul is describing learning contentment through both abundance and need. The verse is about Christ strengthening him to endure changing circumstances, not a promise that every desired achievement will happen.

Keep observation and interpretation distinct

Write down (1) what the text explicitly states, (2) what you infer from it, and (3) where Christians interpret it differently. Check conclusions against the full passage and its historical setting.

Practice: Read Acts 17:10-12 and then study a short passage yourself. What does it say? What does its context clarify? What questions remain?

3. The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit

Christians agree that the Father, Jesus Christ the Son, and the Holy Spirit are central to the New Testament's witness, but Christian traditions explain their relationship in different ways. The word Trinity is a later theological term used by many Christians to summarize their understanding of these passages; other groups interpret some of the same texts differently. Read the passages themselves and note what each explicitly says.

The Father

Jesus addresses God as Father and teaches His disciples to pray to the Father (Matthew 6:9; John 17:1-3). The Father sends the Son into the world (John 3:16-17; Galatians 4:4-6).

The Son — Jesus Christ

Jesus is presented as the Messiah, Savior, and Lord. John 1:1-18 describes the Word's relationship with God and the Word becoming flesh; Colossians 1:15-20 speaks of Christ's supremacy; Hebrews 1:1-4 describes the Son's exalted status. Christians differ over how to express the relationship between the Son and the Father, so read these passages alongside John 17, John 20:28, and 1 Corinthians 8:6.

The Holy Spirit

The Spirit empowers witness (Acts 1:8), teaches and reminds Jesus' disciples (John 14:26), distributes gifts (1 Corinthians 12:4-11), and produces spiritual fruit (Galatians 5:22-23). John 14–16 describes the Spirit as the Helper/Comforter Jesus promised to send.

Read the passages together

  • Matthew 3:13-17: Jesus is baptized, the Spirit descends, and the Father's voice is heard.
  • Matthew 28:19: Jesus names the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in the baptismal commission.
  • John 1:1-18: Consider the Word's identity and relationship with God.
  • John 14:16-17, 26; 15:26; 16:7-15: Study what Jesus says the Spirit does.
  • 1 Corinthians 8:4-6; 2 Corinthians 13:14: Compare how Father, Son, and Spirit are described.

Study fairly: Avoid making a single verse carry the entire argument. Define terms carefully, read the full passage, and represent differing Christian interpretations accurately before deciding what the evidence supports.

4. What Does the Bible Say About the State of the Dead?

Christians differ on whether the dead are conscious with God immediately after death or remain unconscious until a future resurrection. The Bible uses several kinds of language about death, sleep, the soul, and resurrection. This section presents key passages for comparison rather than treating every interpretive question as settled.

Passages often cited for death as sleep or unconsciousness

  • Ecclesiastes 9:5-10: Says the dead know nothing and describes the dead as no longer participating in activities “under the sun.” Read the passage's literary setting and its repeated perspective on life and death.
  • Psalm 146:3-4: Says that when a person's breath departs, their plans perish.
  • John 11:11-14: Jesus describes Lazarus as asleep and then plainly says Lazarus is dead.
  • 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18: Describes believers who have “fallen asleep” being raised at Christ's coming.
  • 1 Corinthians 15:12-26, 51-55: Connects Christian hope with resurrection and the defeat of death.

Passages often cited in support of conscious existence after death

  • Luke 23:39-43: Jesus' words to the repentant criminal are interpreted differently in part because ancient Greek manuscripts did not use modern punctuation.
  • Philippians 1:20-24: Paul speaks of departing and being with Christ; readers debate how this relates to the timing of resurrection.
  • 2 Corinthians 5:1-10: Paul discusses being away from the body and at home with the Lord in a passage about mortality and hope.
  • Revelation 6:9-11: Portrays the souls of martyrs crying out in a symbolic apocalyptic vision.

The shared center of Christian hope

Jesus' resurrection is foundational (1 Corinthians 15:3-8, 12-22), and the New Testament looks forward to resurrection and God's final victory over death. Revelation 21:1-4 pictures a renewed creation where death, mourning, crying, and pain are no more.

Questions to consider: Which passages are poetry, narrative, letters, or visions? What does each say directly? How does each tradition account for both the death-related passages and the resurrection passages?

5. Heaven, Judgment, and Hell in the Bible

The words translated as “heaven,” “hell,” and “eternal” occur in different contexts. Christians have long disagreed about the timing and nature of final punishment, and about how particular biblical words should be translated. It helps to distinguish the present heavenly realm, the hope of life with God, the final judgment, and the ultimate destiny of the wicked.

Heaven and the hope of life with God

  • Matthew 6:9-10: Jesus teaches disciples to pray for God's kingdom to come and His will to be done on earth as in heaven.
  • John 14:1-3: Jesus promises to prepare a place for His followers and to receive them to Himself.
  • 2 Peter 3:13: Looks forward to new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells.
  • Revelation 21:1-4; 22:1-5: Describes the new creation, God's presence with humanity, the end of death and pain, and the healing of the nations.

The Bible's final picture is not merely people escaping earth forever: Revelation ends with a renewed creation and God dwelling with His people. Christians differ on how to relate this future hope to the intermediate state after death.

Judgment and the destiny of the wicked

  • Romans 6:23: Contrasts the wages of sin, death, with God's gift of eternal life in Christ.
  • Matthew 10:28: Speaks of God being able to destroy both soul and body in Gehenna.
  • John 5:28-29: Describes a resurrection to life and a resurrection of judgment.
  • Acts 17:30-31: Says God has appointed a day of judgment through the risen Jesus.
  • Revelation 20:11-15: Depicts final judgment and the lake of fire, called the second death.

Why Christians interpret hell differently

Eternal conscious punishment: Many Christians understand passages such as Matthew 25:46 and Revelation 14:9-11 as teaching ongoing conscious punishment.

Final destruction (often called conditional immortality or annihilationism): Other Christians emphasize passages such as Romans 6:23, Matthew 10:28, John 3:16, and Revelation 20:14-15, understanding the final punishment as destruction and the second death rather than endless conscious life in torment.

Universal reconciliation: Some Christians interpret texts about God's purpose to reconcile all things (for example, Colossians 1:19-20) alongside judgment passages as pointing toward the eventual reconciliation of all. This view is disputed by many other Christians and must also account for warnings of final judgment.

The biblical terms often translated “hell” include Sheol (the realm or state of the dead in the Hebrew Bible), Hades (often the Greek equivalent), Gehenna (used by Jesus in warnings of judgment), and Tartarus (used once in 2 Peter 2:4). They should not automatically be treated as interchangeable in every passage.

Study method: Read the full context of Matthew 25:31-46, Mark 9:42-48, Luke 16:19-31, Romans 6:20-23, 2 Thessalonians 1:5-10, and Revelation 20–22. Notice the imagery, the audience, the outcome described, and how the passage fits with the Bible's account of resurrection and new creation.

6. The Two Greatest Commandments

When Jesus was asked which commandment was greatest, He joined two passages from the Hebrew Scriptures into a concise summary of faithful living.

Love God: “Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind.” — Matthew 22:37; see Deuteronomy 6:4-5.

Love your neighbor: “Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.” — Matthew 22:39; see Leviticus 19:18.

Jesus says that all the Law and the Prophets hang on these two commandments (Matthew 22:34-40). In Matthew 7:12, He also teaches the principle often called the Golden Rule. In John 13:34-35, He tells His disciples to love one another as He has loved them.

What does love look like in practice?

  • Love God: Give God first place, seek to know Him, worship Him sincerely, and respond to His teaching (Deuteronomy 6:6-9; John 14:15).
  • Love your neighbor: Act with justice, mercy, honesty, generosity, and respect (Leviticus 19:9-18; Micah 6:8; James 2:14-17).
  • Love even enemies: Jesus calls His followers to pray for those who mistreat them and to reject retaliation (Matthew 5:43-48; Romans 12:17-21).

Love is not merely a feeling or permission to ignore what is right. In Scripture, love is expressed through faithful action, truth, mercy, and concern for others. Christians debate how particular commandments apply today, but Jesus' summary gives a central test for the way we treat God and people.

Reflect: What would loving God with your whole heart look like this week? Who is your neighbor in a situation where kindness or justice may cost you something?
